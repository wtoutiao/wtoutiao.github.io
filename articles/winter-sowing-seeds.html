Winter Sowing: Start Seeds Outside in the Cold Season

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Winter sowing is the gardener's trick that turns an old milk jug into a tiny free greenhouse. You plant hardy seeds in recycled containers, set them outside in winter, and let snow, rain, and freezing cold do the stratifying for you. By spring you have tough, stocky seedlings — with zero grow lights, zero heat mats, and zero space taken up on your windowsill.

Why Winter Sowing Works

Many hardy flowers and vegetables naturally sprout after a cold period — their seeds need winter chill to wake up, a process called stratification. Winter sowing gives them exactly that, but inside a little jug "greenhouse" that protects them from the worst weather. The jug traps heat on sunny days, lets rain and melting snow moisten the soil, and vents excess moisture so seedlings don't rot. It's cheap, space-saving, and almost impossible to mess up.

The beauty of winter sowing: the weather does the watering and the "start date" for you. There's no hardening-off shock, because the seedlings have lived outside from day one.

What You Need

  • Clear plastic jugs or bottles — milk jugs, juice bottles, or 2-liter soda bottles. Wash them out first.
  • Potting mix — a light, well-drained seed-starting mix (not heavy garden soil).
  • Seeds — choose hardy, cold-tolerant varieties (see below).
  • A knife or scissors to cut the jug, plus a marker and tape.

How to Winter Sow, Step by Step

  1. Prepare the jug — cut it nearly in half around the middle, leaving a hinge of plastic so it opens like a clamshell. Poke 4–6 drainage holes in the bottom.
  2. Fill the bottom with 3–4 inches of moistened potting mix.
  3. Sow your seeds per the packet depth, then firm the soil gently. Don't overfill — 6–12 seeds per jug is plenty.
  4. Label it with the plant name and date using a permanent marker or a taped label.
  5. Close the top and tape it shut, leaving the cap off or slightly open for air and drainage.
  6. Set it outside in a sheltered spot that gets sun — against a south wall or along a fence works well.
  7. Ignore it until spring. On warm days, open the top to vent if it's steaming inside.
Watering is almost free — melting snow and rain soak in through the open top. Only add water if a jug looks bone-dry after a dry stretch.

What to Sow in Winter

Choose hardy, cold-tolerant plants. Great picks for beginners include perennial flowers, wildflowers, and many vegetables:

  • Flowers — marigolds, zinnias, calendula, poppies, daisies, and many native wildflowers (great for a pollinator garden).
  • Vegetables — lettuce, spinach, kale, arugula, and other leafy greens that love cool weather.
  • Herbs — parsley, cilantro, dill, and chives start easily this way.

Check your seed packets: anything that says "direct sow in early spring" or "needs cold stratification" is a good winter-sowing candidate. Warm-season crops like tomatoes and peppers are better started indoors — see microgreens and our indoor guides for year-round growing.

When to Expect Sprouts & How to Plant Them Out

Expect the first seedlings in late winter to early spring, as days warm. Once they're a couple inches tall and have a few true leaves, start opening the jug on mild days to harden them off further. When the risk of hard frost has passed, transplant them into the garden or containers just like any seedling — they'll be sturdier than most store-bought starts because they've grown tough from the beginning.

Why You'll Love It

  • It's free — recycled jugs and a bag of potting mix are all you need.
  • No special equipment — no lights, mats, or indoor shelf space.
  • Less watering — snow and rain do most of it.
  • Hardier plants — seedlings that never knew a windowsill are naturally wind- and sun-toughened.

Winter sowing turns the quietest season into a head start. Grab a few old jugs, sow some hardy seeds, and let the cold do the work — spring will bring you a whole tray of free, sturdy plants ready for the garden.
