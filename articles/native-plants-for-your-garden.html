Native Plants for Your Garden: Why They Matter

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

Native plants are having a moment — and for good reason. They're adapted to your local climate, need less water and care, and support the insects and birds that keep gardens healthy. Here's how to make the switch.

What Are Native Plants?

Native plants are species that grow naturally in your region, without human help. Because they've adapted to local soil, rainfall, and weather, they thrive with far less input than exotic ornamentals.

Why Gardeners Are Choosing Natives

  • Less water — natives are drought-tolerant once established
  • Less maintenance — no fussy feeding or spraying
  • Wildlife support — they feed local pollinators and birds
  • Climate resilience — they survive local extremes better

Easy Native Plants to Start With

Across much of North America, reliable beginners include purple coneflower, black-eyed Susan, butterfly milkweed, goldenrod, and wild bergamot. Check your local nursery for species native to your specific region — that's the key.

How to Plant Them

Plant natives in the same spot and season you would any perennial: in well-drained soil with appropriate sun. Water regularly through the first year while roots establish, then cut back on water — they're designed to cope on their own.

Mix Natives With Food Crops

Natives don't have to replace your vegetable garden. Plant a native border to attract pollinators, which boosts your tomatoes, peppers, and squash. Everybody wins.
