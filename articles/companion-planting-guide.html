Companion Planting: 12 Pairings That Actually Work

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Companion planting gets a lot of hype — and a lot of myths. The truth is a handful of pairings genuinely help by repelling pests, attracting beneficial insects, or improving growth, while many "classic" combos do nothing. Here's the practical, evidence-minded guide.

How Companion Planting Really Works

Good companion pairings work through four mechanisms: repelling pests (strong-smelling herbs confuse or deter insects), attracting beneficials (flowers pull in pollinators and pest-eaters), physical support (tall crops shade or shade-sensitive ones), and space efficiency (deep-rooted and shallow-rooted crops share a bed without competing). It's not magic — it's smart layout.

Tomatoes + Basil

The most famous pairing, and one of the few with real evidence. Basil's strong scent helps mask tomatoes from pests, and some studies suggest basil improves tomato flavor and growth. Plant basil among tomato rows — it's also a handy place to grab leaves for cooking. See our tomato guide for growing basics.

Carrots + Onions

Onions and their relatives help deter the carrot root fly, one of the most damaging carrot pests, by masking the carrot's scent. Interplant rows of carrots and onions, and you'll see fewer damaged roots. This is one of the most reliable pest-repelling pairings in home gardens.

Marigolds + Just About Everything

Marigolds are the workhorse of companion planting. Their roots release compounds that deter root nematodes, and their bright flowers pull in pollinators and hoverflies (which eat aphids). Plant a ring of marigolds around vegetable beds or tuck them between rows. Our easy flowers guide covers them.

Corn + Beans + Squash ("Three Sisters")

The classic Native American trio: corn gives beans a climbing support, beans fix nitrogen into the soil for all three, and squash's large leaves shade the ground to suppress weeds and keep moisture in. It's efficient, productive, and genuinely works when you plant them together in a mound. See our squash guide for the growing details.

Lettuce + Radishes + Tall Crops

Lettuce thrives in the partial shade of taller plants like tomatoes or beans, staying cooler and longer in summer. Radishes grow fast and can be harvested before the lettuce fills in — a perfect quick-and-slow succession pairing. It's a space-efficiency win, not a pest trick. Our lettuce guide explains the basics.

Herbs That Repel Pests

Rosemary, sage, and thyme help deter cabbage moths and carrot flies when planted near brassicas. Mint repels ants and aphids but is invasive — always grow it in a sunken pot so it doesn't take over. Dill and fennel attract wasps that eat pests, but keep dill away from carrots and fennel away from dill, as they cross-pollinate poorly.

What to Avoid: The Combinations That Hurt

A few pairings genuinely fight each other. Onions and beans — onions inhibit bean growth. Potatoes and tomatoes — both are nightshades and share diseases (blight), so keep them far apart. Fennel and most plants — fennel secretes chemicals that stunt its neighbors; give it its own corner. And don't plant brassicas (cabbage, broccoli) where radish relatives grew the year before, to avoid disease buildup.

The Simplest Way to Start

Don't overthink it. If you're a beginner, just do three things: plant marigolds around the bed, interplant onions with carrots, and put basil next to your tomatoes. That covers the three most reliable wins with almost no extra effort. For everything else, pair by space and light needs rather than chasing every myth.
